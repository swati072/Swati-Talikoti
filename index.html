dynamic sweep sensitivity insight

A professional mechanical designer never evaluates static numbers in a vacuum. Tuning the min/max sweep inputs above visualizes the sensitivity derivatives of your gear set. Watch how changing module boundaries, shaft speeds, or pinion teeth immediately charts real-time physical curve trends below.

1. Stress vs Module Laws

Bending stress is modeled as a cantilever beam, where tooth thickness relates directly to the Normal Module (m).

σ = Ft / (b * m * Y)

Because m is in the denominator, scaling it up spreads the tangential force across a larger profile depth, dropping bending stress exponentially.

2. Force vs Teeth Count

With fixed input power and speed, torque remains fully static. Tangential force (Ft) represents the action of this torque at the pitch radius.

Ft = 2000 * T / (m * Z)

As the teeth number (Z) increases, the pitch diameter expands, enlarging the moment arm of transmission and dropping the tangential shearing force required to carry specified horsepower.

3. Torque vs Shaft Speed

Gears transmit horsepower, which is a multiplication of rotational velocity (ω) and torque (T).

T = (P * 60,000) / (2π * N)

Torque shares a clean hyperbolic inverse affinity to Speed (RPM). Standard high-velocity gearboxes require much lower operational torques, allowing for lighter, compact gear face structures.

The analyzer carries out the absolute mathematical equations taught in physical engineering classrooms:

1
Velocity Reduction & Kinematics Check

Evaluates perfect gear mesh speed reduction. The gear ratio divides input RPM to find secondary velocity: N2 = N1 / i, and multiplies torque proportionally.

Major Mechanical Failure Modes

Professional powertrains must guard against two distinct material yield modes. Failure to secure a safety factor ≥ 1.5 leads to severe dynamic breakdowns.

1. Pinion Bending Tension Fracture

Root stress behavior: When teeth mesh, the entire load applies to the gear tip, acting like a cantilever beam. This creates maximum tensile stress concentration at the root fillet.

Fatigue Cracking: Continuous load cycling propagates root-crack fissures, ultimately causing complete shear breakage of individual teeth.

2. Surface Contact Fatigue (Pitting)

Hertzian compression: Extreme contact pressure between curved loading faces sets up intense shear forces directly under the steel surface.

Surface Cratering: Fatigue produces microscopic sub-surface cracks that pop completely off, leaving surface crater pits around the active pitch line.
